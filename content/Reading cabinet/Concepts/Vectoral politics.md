## Theoretical intervention

This article is written in the very abstruse idiom of High Cultural Studies (not a criticism).

It's a response to theories of capitalism that rely (in Wark's telling) on an incomplete reading of Marx, in which:

* the only two classes that matter are "capital" and "labor"
* "labor" contains many subgroups within it, but little attention is paid to capital
* labor, or "some force coming from below" \[page 03] is the only way to disrupt capitalism
* labor is unfortunately too enfeebled to disrupt capitalism in its current form

Consequently, these readings (Wark does not specify who is making them) lead to a dogmatic assumption that "everything can or will be disrupted except capitalism itself." This benefits capitalism's flagbearers as much as its detractors! 

Wark's intervention is to say that actually, capitalism can be, and is at present, disrupted by a new ruling class that is neither "capital" nor "labor." This ruling class derives its power from controlling and gating **information**. It is in conflict with the existing ruling classes in her reading, landlords and capitalists.

## Landlords and capitalists

Citing the work of economist David Ricardo, Wark notes that there are two types of ruling classes: the landlords, and the capitalists (or what I would say as the industrialists). 

| The landlord                                                                                                                                                   | The capitalist industrialist                                                                                                                      |
| -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| profits from rent                                                                                                                                              | profits from industry                                                                                                                             |
| captures surplus land                                                                                                                                          | extracts resources from the land                                                                                                                  |
| "solid" (land is fixed)                                                                                                                                        | "liquid" (flow of items, industry, resources, labor)                                                                                              |
| severs ties between particular people and particular places/rights/land - peasants are no longer tied to a particular estate, they are no longer "of" the land | severs ties between the land and what is "within" the land - coal extracted from the ground is shipped off to very distant places to be processed |

When Wark describes the industrialists as creating a "built environment that no longer follows and forms the contours of the land" \[page 4] I think of the full-bleed illustrations in Kate Beaton's memoir [[Ducks]], where the Alberta oil sands are transformed into a strange, liminal space, chopping up the land into parcels to be worked, covered over in monstrously huge industrial equipment. 

The capitalist, however, has some obvious weak spots: 

> Industry relied on the constant flow of resources, of coal and raw materials, and of finished goods speeding off to market. Those flows could be disrupted at key choke points. Second nature was a slender network — ports and rail — of big things — mines and factories — and could be shut down at its weakest link. Organized labor achieved its victories \[...] through the actual or potential use of this power to shut down the infrastructure of second nature.

In Wark's narration, what ultimately allowed capitalists to "prevail" over labor in the 1980s (automation, deskilling, decline of unions, etc) was information technology. IT makes it possible to reroute away from choke points, to easily find and use cheaper and more pliable sources of labor overseas, etc. 

But capitalists do not control the information itself, and that is a critical limit on their power. Those who control and produce information are a new ruling class: the vectoralist class.

## "Vectoralists" redefine information as property they control

The metaphor behind the 'vectoralist' class is a little tortured and ultimately I don't find it that helpful, but the idea is that while the industrialist is tied to a fixed built environment (capital investments, shipping lines) the vectoralist is operating in a plane where anything can be connected to anything, because information is free of material limitations:

>information becomes separated from its material strata, and can be effectively channeled anywhere on the planet, stored at negligible cost, processed easily into complex patterns. \[page 05]

But anyways. The vectoralist makes money by producing information and then employing new intellectual property regimes to ensure that information (which should be free-flowing) belongs to only them. The vectoralist does not produce things but produces information *about* things — GIS maps, patented processes, logistics data, consumer data, driving data, that direct how everything else should unfold. The vectoralist relies on intellectual property to keep this information within its moat. It relies on having information no one else does.

For example: GM wants Apple CarPlay out of its cars because they want to produce and control information about what people do with the infotainment system. 

Another (possible) example: Uber's value is not from the service it provides but the data hoovered up as part of providing that service

Another example: OpenAI and Anthropic have, in an LLM, the ultimate "separation of information from its material strata." The masses of training data are proprietary (I think), the precise weighting of the model is proprietary, the RLHF processes are proprietary. Of course, it also needs the landlords' cooperation — land for data centers is scarce. 

She also says they make their money not on profit, but *interest,* which "means not just the return on the investment of information in the form of money but any surplus information, acquired through unequal exchanges of information" \[page 05]. So this tells me that she's not making an argument about the sale of data, but rather that the companies are highly *valued* in the market based on the strength and scope of the data they have collected and hoard.
## Hackers: the labor class

Capital has labor and landlords have peasants - vectoralists have hackers, who actually produce the information. I thought this section was a little more abstract, BUT it's interesting how she calls out 4 ways that vectoralists subordinate hackers:
- cultivation of a small elite (the software engineers who get huge stock bonuses)
- "routinization" via object-oriented programming and other ways of making the infrastructure/work of producing information into discrete, chunkedparts
- "in-sourcing" - this, I *think,* means going around the hacker to mine the open Internet/"commons of information" (I think here of mining something like Reddit posts) or inducing users to provide data for free. 
- criminalizing the hacker (intellectual property regimes)

## How we understand capitalism and change

I really thought this article was worth the effort of reading. I think in 2026, people (me) are implicitly aware that everything is becoming "vectorized," meaning everything is becoming turned into something with data markers that can be flagged, used, manipulated, and hoarded. But this article is more than that, because it is making a strong intervention into how we understand capitalism as a historical force:

> Maybe \[our current society is] still an exploitative and class-ridden society—just not exactly or entirely a capitalist one. Maybe a fair bit of it could still fairly described as capitalism, but perhaps there's some new, if no less exploitative, form accreting on top of it.
> 
> \[...]
> 
> Historical change is a matter of multiple classes and class conflicts; perhaps other classes besides capital and labor could be thought of as driving agents of historical change; and perhaps even within capitalism a new kind of class conflict based on a new mode of production could emerge.

Invoking "late stage capitalism" as the current regime that is always the same and from which we will never be free—is a cynic's move. It suggests knowingness but actually reveals a paucity of historical thinking. As a historian, [[Things were different in the past and they may be different again|I generally think it is valuable to know that things were once different because it creates space to believe they can be different and change again]]. I am also usually skeptical of anything being described as [[Claiming "inevitability" is a sales tactic|inevitable]]. 

This is getting at a slightly different idea. Inevitability is a claim about how change (on someone else's terms) must happen and we have to accept it. Whenever something is described as *unchanging*, that is also a claim—about how change is **no**t possible and the status quo is all that we can ask for. It is also a way of foreclosing the future.

Unrelated: I'm also intrigued by her first paragraph—that "disruption" is a "nihilistic" inversion of the old Marx phrase that capitalism sweeps away old social forms. Want to think about what work is happening when we say something will be "disrupted." You can't shake a stick in corporate without something being on the edge of massive "disruption."
