Henry Grabar, *Paved Paradise: How Parking Explains the World* (Penguin, 2024)

Key takeaways:

- Urban planners in the mid 20th century misguidedly thought that the solution to the problem of declining downtowns was that the downtowns didn’t have enough parking, so they hollowed out the downtown core to provide parking. But actually this hastened the decline further and robbed downtowns of the density that parking prevents
- “Free parking” is a kind of “superficial access” to the city. Parking for free seems like it’s more equitable, but zoom outward and it’s not - it’s a “consolation prize” for people who got priced out of downtowns and pushed to the “car dependent periphery”
    - Instead of asking why college students can’t park for free on campus - why do they feel they can’t live by campus?
    - This from the conclusion
- Free parking serves cities in a perverse way - it creates chaos that incentivizes drivers to break the law, and then cities collect money off the tickets. If you have metered parking, it leads to fewer tickets because people usually can find a space and therefore they don’t double park
- Parking minimums were a disastrous attempt by govts to compel builders to build parking. But parking ups the cost of projects and limits how much you can put on a piece of land (eg, ADUs don’t work with parking minimums) - so it reduces density and makes affordable housing projects untenable
- The perversity of allowing public space - which is what the streets are! - to store cars. Choices about parking are choices about who the city is for and what it values
- Control of parking is control of SPACE. Is the space parking? Or is it a bike lane? An outdoor restaurant? A baseball diamond?
- From the conclusion: “making it harder to park might make it easier to not drive” (280)

  

**My takeaway: the ubiquity of parking is a sign of vast imaginative impoverishment that we can’t think of anything else to do with our space than use it to store cars**
