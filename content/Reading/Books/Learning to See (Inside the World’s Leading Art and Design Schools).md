Keith Sawyer, *Learning to See: Inside the World's Leading Art and Design Schools* (MIT Press, 2025)

Analysis of the creative process that working and teaching artists (art professors at top art schools) teach their students and use in their own process - that is successful at generating art.

5 principles of the creative process:

1. **Process** itself. Professors teach their students to “trust that the process of exploration will lead you to a good problem and a good solution” (p.66) and encourage students to not think about the final output. Making art is not about having a great idea and then executing it ruthlessly to the end—that method suggests that the quality of the work is wholly based on the quality of the initial starting idea. Instead, making art is about beginning somewhere and iterating and exploring. The quality of the work is based on the quality of the engagement with the actual artistic process.
2. Dialogue & engagement with **materials**. Professors encourage students to get in and start playing with materials (paper, ink, paint, type) so they can start exploring how the qualities of the materials themselves change their thinking and their approach to the problem or the solution.
3. They encourage students to lean into **emergence - showing that “meaning emerges from the process of making” (page 132) rather than whatever meaning they intended to create at the beginning. See this great quote on page 120:**
    1. If the painting means at the end what you want it to mean at the beginning, to me there’s something wrong with that. That means you didn’t learn anything. You didn’t grow at all. The painting should grow with you. You should be educating yourself while you’re working.
4. They recognize that leaning into these new, emergent definitions and ideas may mean “failure”—but failure is not a bad thing, and in fact they encourage students to fail productively early on.
5. And finally, **seeing**: seeing the potential in the work they are doing (”what’s working”) and learning to see whether their “intentions match what [they have] generated” (p. 168). So seeing is about the alignment between decisions in the studio and final outcomes.

  

Then the middle section is part of the how—using project assignments that mix constraint and ambiguity.


The last section is about “studio talk” and the thinking processes professors model for students during studio critique. The biggest one is that they show students how to animate the work, see it as something separate from themselves, and enter into a dialogue with it. They are teaching students how “to listen to what the work is telling us to do.”

Key to this is…

Seeing the work as having its own energy, needs, and propulsion - separate from the student

Individual elements of the work are acting (”I think this part here is asking for…”) in concert with one another — “creative animation shows the student that her work is a collection of agentive elements which can interact with each other independent from her.”
