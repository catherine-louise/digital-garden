I sometimes worry that our society has lost the sense that *things were not always this way.* I don't mean that in a sense of, "things used to be better," or "things used to be worse," which are value judgments. I simply mean that they were different. The world was once a different place. We used to do things differently. People thought differently, and saw the world differently, and communicated differently. 

There is always pat nostalgia (RIP Buzzfeed) to remind you that once phones were chunky and television only had three channels. But difference, true difference, is weird and strange. There is a book I read in graduate school called *The Woman Beneath the Skin* by Barbara Duden. Duden analyzes an 18th (17th? don't remember, not sure where the book is in my house right now to check) century doctor's notebook only to discover that the ailing women who came to this doctor described their body in ways so completely different than we do today. They saw their body as made up of *flows*, that their ailments were the result of flows being blocked or flows being misguided. How different from the model we have where the body is visualized as systems (the skeletal system, the muscular system). Fascinating! 

As I'm writing this I think: well, future generations will see the world differently. Future generations will be used to the technological developments that I, in my particular moment of historical time, find unnerving. Is that not the logic that Silicon Valley uses to tell us that AI is *happening*, it is perhaps even [[Historical thinking part 3 - Nothing is inevitable, everything is contingent|inevitable]], and everyone else will just have to *get used to it*? 

But I am not making a claim here (in this writing) about historical change. My claim is more about context and situating the past in its own terms. And, in fact, when you understand that the past was different, and actually investigate what that means, you can see that people in the past also assumed certain things would be inevitable. If those assumptions were wrong, and often they were, it was not because people in the past were stupid or misguided, but because they were subject, just as we are now, to the context of their time, shaping their perspective on 
what was important, what was worth paying attention to, what was possible and impossible.

I also worry that this is all boiling down to something pat about empathy, and having empathy for those who were different—and there *is* a particular way historians use and create empathy, which is possible through deep understanding of context. But what I'm thinking about in this context is the idea of tact, [[On tact in dark times|as Corina Stan discusses in an article for Aeon.]] European thinkers, struggling to understand what morality would look like after World War II, formulated an idea of tact as "as a thoughtful calibration of distance." Tact would repair the social order by not seeing individuals as property, materiel in a war machine, specimens to be assessed and categorized—the view of the world that made the war's horrors possible—but by making space to recognize, even celebrate, their nuanced differences. Is it then, that historical thinking is about also cultivating a sense of tact, of nuanced attention? Is it a form of repair?
