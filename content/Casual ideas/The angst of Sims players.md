I grew up playing *The Sims* and *The Sims 2*, but *The Sims 4* is different than both. It comes covered in the fingerprints of corporate software development. 
## A world of features

The joke of *The Sims* has always been that you buy the base game and then a thousand dollars' worth of expansion packs, but in *The Sims 4* you can actually rack up that much with 112 pieces of DLC. There are Expansion Pack, Game Packs, Stuff Packs, and Kits. Sims players (on reddit, at least) complain that "a single Sims 2 expansion pack had way more content than a Sims 4 pack" but what I think is interesting is how standardized the *Sims 4* expansions are. Every expansion pack releases with four features. They usually can be identified by how they are written: Sims can become Landlords or Tenants, which means being a landlord is a new feature, and they can live on Apartment Lots, which means there's a new lot type, and that new lot type is a feature. 

A product is made up of discrete, named features. The feature is how the design vision or concept—a fairy pack, an apartments pack, a family life pack—gets extruded into something that a large organization can design, build, and deploy. They are the actual gameplay that you get. Players want a pack themed around apartment living. They really want the ability to build apartments, but an expansion pack needs gameplay features to be viable, so I imagine a cross-functional team ideated about what kind of gameplay could go along with this expansion, and what existing architecture they could leverage to cut down the development lift, and so Sims got the ability to become landlords or tenants. A few months later the next pack idea is to allow people to create in-home businesses. This previously was *not* viable, because it would be too much of a developer lift and require too many changes, but now (thanks to the previous apartment pack) the team already has created a feature that allows players to toggle whether or not a room within a lot is private or public. So the in-home business feature gets built on top of that. They have to find a place for the in-home businesses to go in the UI - where is the control panel for running your business? Let's find a place for it to go. It can go where you also manage your vet clinic, if you have one, or your landlord business. These poor UI elements are so overloaded with information, but 10 years into the Sims 4, it's the only place for things to go.

## Raging and angsting at EA
This idea might just be borne from me observing people in the Sims community get very upset about their Sims games, but there is a particular sort of affective angst here - Sims players desperately wanting types of gameplay that will probably not happen because they require ways of thinking about the game that cut across features, and specifically, the *feature* as developed and practiced in modern software development. 

It's possible that the game architecture/software just isn't robust enough to support what players desire. And my casual observation of Reddit threads, Sims content creators, and Twitch livestream chats is skewing my perspective towards the very vocal, the very committed, the very invested. The game still makes money! 

But I am thinking about [["The feature" is a unit of analysis worth considering|*the feature as unit of analysis*]] is because:
- modern corporate software is built on features, and the Sims 4 is no different: it is built feature by feature
- features come with particular forms, genres, logics, etc., built in. 
- features—their deployment, their updates, their roadmap—are how players understand and assume EA cares, or does not care, about the game
	- players themselves would say "bugs," eg, "broken game" - but for bugs to get fixed, that's often fixing a feature or it's prioritized in the manner of a feature that has to be written in a precise way in order to get it into a development team's queue

I suppose my last thought right now is that analyses of games, or software, that just focus on the text of the thing, (e.g., [[Break the Aspiration Meter!]]) not only miss the interactivity that shapes how a game actually unfolds (the player's role), but also ignore the material conditions in which these gameplay loops are produced.
